Resume & Profile

A recruiter often spends under a minute on a first read. Make that minute count.

Resume checklist

  • One or two pages, simple layout, readable font, no photos or graphics that confuse scanners.
  • A short summary at the top: who you are, what you do, what you want next.
  • A skills section grouped clearly (for example: Cloud, Testing, Security, Tools).
  • Bullets that show results: "Cut report time from 4 hours to 30 minutes" beats "Responsible for reports".
  • Keywords from the job posting, used honestly, so applicant tracking systems can find you.
  • Dates and job titles that are consistent and easy to follow.
  • No typos. Read it aloud, then ask a friend to read it.

Writing strong bullets

Start with a verb

Built, automated, reduced, led, tested, migrated, secured.

Add a number

Time saved, money saved, users served, bugs found, uptime gained.

Name the tool

Say which tools you used, so the reader knows your hands-on skills.

If you are changing careers or have gaps

  • Lead with a summary that explains your direction in one sentence.
  • Highlight transferable skills such as communication, problem solving and ownership.
  • List courses, certifications and personal projects as real experience.
  • Be honest about gaps and say briefly what you did: caring for family, studying, freelancing.

Online profile

  • Use a clear, friendly photo and a headline that says what you do.
  • Match your profile to your resume: same titles, same dates.
  • Ask former colleagues or managers for short recommendations.
  • For technical roles, keep a public GitHub with a few clean, documented projects.

Interview Prep

Good preparation turns nerves into confidence.

Before the interview

  • Research the company: what it does, who its customers are, recent news.
  • Re-read the job description and match each requirement to one of your own examples.
  • Prepare three to five stories from your work. Use the STAR method below.
  • Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask them.
  • Test your camera, microphone and internet for video calls. Plan your route and arrive 10 minutes early for in-person ones.
  • Choose your outfit the night before and keep a copy of your resume handy.

The STAR method

S: Situation

Set the scene in one or two sentences.

T: Task

What was your responsibility or goal?

A: Action

What did you do, step by step?

R: Result

What changed? Use numbers if you can.

Example. Situation: Our nightly test run kept failing and blocked releases. Task: I was asked to find out why and stop it happening again. Action: I traced the failures to two unstable tests, rewrote them and added a retry report. Result: Failures dropped by 90% and releases went out on time for the next quarter.

Technical interviews (IT, QA, DevOps, security)

  • Explain your thinking out loud. Interviewers want to hear how you solve problems.
  • Know the basics of every tool on your resume. Expect questions on anything you list.
  • It is fine to say "I do not know, but here is how I would find out."
  • Practice a short walkthrough of one project you built: goal, design, problems, result.
  • For hands-on tests, read the question twice, ask clarifying questions, then start.

On the day

  • Smile, greet everyone, and keep answers to about two minutes.
  • Listen fully before you answer. Pausing for a moment is fine.
  • Be honest. Never claim skills you do not have.
  • If asked about salary early, say you would like to learn more about the role first, then give a researched range.

After the interview

  • Send a short thank-you email within 24 hours that mentions something you discussed.
  • Write down the questions you were asked and how you did, for next time.
  • If you hear nothing by the date they gave, send one polite follow-up.

Job Search

A steady, organized search beats sending hundreds of random applications.

A simple weekly plan

  • Set a target: for example 5 quality applications and 3 conversations per week.
  • Tailor your resume to each role instead of sending the same one everywhere.
  • Tell friends, family and former colleagues what you are looking for. Many jobs are filled by referral.
  • Track every application in a simple sheet.
  • Follow up politely after one week if you have not heard back.
  • Reserve time each week to learn something new for your field.

Where to look

  • Company career pages for places you would like to work.
  • General job boards and professional networking sites.
  • Local job centers, community groups, meetups and career fairs.
  • Recruiters who work in your field. Send them a short, clear message and your resume.

Networking without feeling awkward

  • Ask for advice, not a job. People like to share what they know.
  • Keep your message short: who you are, why you are writing, one specific question.
  • Thank people and tell them how it went.

Offers and negotiation

  • Thank them, ask for the offer in writing, and take a day or two to review it.
  • Look beyond salary: benefits, leave, training, remote options and growth.
  • It is normal to ask politely whether there is flexibility.

Stay motivated

Rejection is part of the process and says little about your worth. Keep learning, keep practicing, and celebrate small wins like every interview you complete.
